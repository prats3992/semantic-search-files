# Exoplanet Discovery: Methods and Milestones

## What is an Exoplanet?
An exoplanet, or extrasolar planet, is a planet outside our Solar System, orbiting a star other than our Sun. The first confirmation of an exoplanet orbiting a main-sequence star was made in 1995, when a giant planet was found in a four-day orbit around the nearby star 51 Pegasi. Thousands of exoplanets have been discovered since, ranging in size from gas giants larger than Jupiter to small rocky planets like Earth.

## Methods of Detection

Several methods are used to discover and confirm exoplanets. Each method has its strengths and weaknesses and often provides different kinds of information about the planet.

### 1. Radial Velocity (Doppler Spectroscopy)
This method detects the slight wobble of a star caused by the gravitational pull of an orbiting planet. As the star moves towards and away from Earth, its light spectrum shifts (Doppler effect). This was the first successful method for finding exoplanets.
*   **Pros:** Can determine a planet's minimum mass and orbital period. Effective for massive planets close to their stars.
*   **Cons:** Biased towards detecting massive planets. The inclination of the orbit is usually unknown, so only a minimum mass can be determined. Requires high-resolution spectrographs.

### 2. Transit Photometry
This method observes the slight dimming of a star's light as an orbiting planet passes in front of it (a transit). This is currently the most prolific method, largely thanks to space telescopes like Kepler and TESS.
*   **Pros:** Can determine the planet's size (radius), orbital period, and sometimes atmospheric composition if combined with spectroscopy during transit. Can detect smaller planets.
*   **Cons:** Requires the planet's orbit to be aligned edge-on to our line of sight. Transits are rare for planets with long orbital periods. Susceptible to false positives (e.g., eclipsing binary stars).

### 3. Direct Imaging
This involves directly taking pictures of exoplanets. It is extremely challenging because planets are much fainter than their host stars and are very close to them.
*   **Pros:** Provides direct confirmation and allows for spectral analysis of the planet's atmosphere, revealing its composition and temperature. Can study planets far from their stars.
*   **Cons:** Technically very difficult. Works best for young, massive planets that are still glowing hot from formation and are far from their host stars.

### 4. Gravitational Microlensing
This method relies on the gravitational field of a star (the lens star) bending the light of a more distant background star. If the lens star has a planet, the planet's own gravity can cause a brief, additional brightening or distortion of the lensed light.
*   **Pros:** Can detect planets at great distances from Earth, including free-floating planets (rogue planets) not orbiting any star. Sensitive to low-mass planets.
*   **Cons:** Microlensing events are rare and non-repeating, making follow-up observations difficult. Provides limited information about the planet's properties.

### 5. Astrometry
This method precisely measures a star's position in the sky and observes how it changes over time due to the gravitational pull of an orbiting planet. It's similar to the radial velocity method but detects the star's side-to-side motion instead of its motion towards or away from us.
*   **Pros:** Can determine a planet's true mass and orbital inclination. Good for detecting planets with long orbital periods.
*   **Cons:** Requires extremely precise measurements over long periods. Best suited for massive planets far from their stars. The Gaia space observatory is expected to find many exoplanets using this method.

## Key Milestones
*   **1992:** First confirmed discovery of exoplanets orbiting a pulsar (PSR B1257+12).
*   **1995:** Discovery of 51 Pegasi b, the first exoplanet found orbiting a main-sequence star.
*   **1999:** First detection of an exoplanet transit (HD 209458 b).
*   **2001:** First atmospheric detection for an exoplanet (HD 209458 b, sodium detected).
*   **2004:** First direct image of an exoplanet candidate (2M1207b).
*   **2009:** Launch of NASA's Kepler Space Telescope, which revolutionized exoplanet discovery using the transit method.
*   **2017:** Discovery of the TRAPPIST-1 system, with seven Earth-sized planets, some in the habitable zone.
*   **2018:** Launch of NASA's Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS), an all-sky survey mission.
*   **Ongoing:** Continued discoveries and characterization efforts, including the search for biosignatures on potentially habitable exoplanets.

## The Search for Habitable Exoplanets
The ultimate goal for many in exoplanet research is to find planets that could support life. This involves looking for planets within the "habitable zone" of their star, where temperatures could allow liquid water to exist on the surface. Future telescopes like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and upcoming ground-based extremely large telescopes will play a crucial role in characterizing the atmospheres of these potentially habitable worlds.
